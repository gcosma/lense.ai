Getting started: registering and logging in

Lense.ai runs in the browser at lense.up.railway.app — nothing to install, and free to use. Registration requires an access code, provided on request: you register once with your email, the code, and a personal password of your own — then log in with your email and personal password.

FeatureWhat it does
1The workspace panelLense.ai is a workspace for exploring Prevention of Future Deaths reports. The tool is free to use; registration requires an access code, provided on request — Lense.ai is a research project, and registration helps us understand how the tool is used and demonstrate its impact. The contact for requesting a code is on the page itself; More about the tool opens the project website, and the User guide button at the far end of the same line opens this guide.
2Log in / Register tabsFirst visit: Register. Every visit after that: Log in.
3RegisteringRegister with your email address to create your own personal account — it is what keeps your projects and uploads private to you and the colleagues you invite. Colleagues can only share projects with you once you have registered, using the address you registered with.
4New personal passwordChoose your own password for the account. Show reveals what you have typed.
5Password we emailed youThe access code we provide on request, sent to you by email. It is needed once, at registration. If you do not have one, request it from the contact shown on the page.
6Logging in & forgotten passwordsAfter registering, log in with your email and personal password. Forgotten your password? on the Log in tab resets it.
After you are in. The menu on the left of every page takes you to the archive (Home), the charts (Analysis & dashboard), your Projects, and the review and analysis pages (Factor annotation, Factor analysis, Theme annotation, Theme analysis) — each covered by its own sheet in this guide series.

The Home page at a glance

Home is the archive of Prevention of Future Deaths reports. Each numbered marker on the picture is explained in the table beneath. Advanced search is pictured on page 2; clicking a report’s title opens the report window, covered on pages 3–4.

FeatureWhat it does
1MenuMoves between the tool’s pages: Home (this archive), Analysis & dashboard (charts), Projects, Factor annotation, Factor analysis, Theme annotation and Theme analysis — each with its own guide sheet. A Documentation section at the foot links out to the Human factors evaluation sheet.
2Reports indexedThe size of the archive — every PFD report captured from judiciary.uk to date. Every night the tool re-checks judiciary.uk, picking up newly published reports and responses added to existing reports (every report from the last 12 months is re-visited, so a response published months later is still captured). The archive keeps itself current.
3Added recently — 24 H / Week / MonthWhat’s new — how many reports arrived in the last day, week and month. Click a tile to show only those new reports in the table below: the quickest way to see the latest arrivals. The tiles count fixed windows — the last 24 hours, week and month, refreshed by the nightly update. Click again or clear filters to return to everything.
4SearchKeyword search finds your exact words; Advanced search searches by meaning: reports are ranked by how closely they match the search phrase or topic, not only its exact words. A pill shows the match count; ✕ clears the search. In Advanced search the tool also shows the expanded topic it searched (so you see exactly what was looked for), Load more brings further results, and each row’s Relevance badge says why it matched — Keyword (exact words) or Semantic (similar meaning).
5FiltersNarrow by Category, by healthcare speciality, by location, and by date — the date of report (when the coroner wrote it, not when it appeared online) or the date of death. Two switches shape the view. A Category system switch chooses the category vocabulary: Judiciary — the categories reports are published under on the judiciary website (Hospital deaths, Suicide…) — or NHS, which re-classifies each report into the NHS reference categories (Ambulance, Mental Health, Community…); it governs both the Category filter and the Category shown in the table. A separate Healthcare speciality filter (Emergency Department, Cardiac, Mental Health – Community…) narrows to a clinical area and applies under either category system. A second switch chooses how location is filtered — by NHS England region (the seven regions) or by Coroner (area and name). “Active filters” shows everything applied; remove any from there.
6Column headingsClick any heading to sort the table by it; click again to reverse the order.
7A report’s titleOpens the report window: full text with search words highlighted, key facts, evidence, and the original documents — explained on pages 3–4. A report may carry more than one category; all show in the Category column.
8Tick boxesSelect reports; a bar appears with the count and Download ZIP — the original PDFs of everything selected, in one file.
9ResponsesHow many response documents are published alongside the report on judiciary.uk. Kept up to date by the same nightly check (see 2): a response added later to a report from the last 12 months appears here after that night’s check. Open the report (7) to see and download them.

Advanced search: searching by meaning

A real comparison: “home birth” as a keyword search finds 9 reports containing those exact words. The same phrase in Advanced search finds 192 — because it searches by meaning, reaching reports about planned home deliveries, obstetric emergencies at home or delayed transfers to hospital that never use the phrase itself.

FeatureWhat it does
1Advanced search tabEnter a search phrase or topic. Advanced search matches by meaning, so reports that use different words for the same thing are found too.
2Found … reportsHow many reports match your topic. Results arrive in pages — Load more brings the next set.
3Expanded topicThe tool shows exactly what it searched for: your phrase expanded into the related terms and situations it looked for (planned home delivery, domiciliary childbirth, delayed transfer from home to hospital…). You always know what the results represent.
4Relevance badgeWhy each report matched: Keyword — it contains your exact words; Semantic — it is about your topic but worded differently. Semantic matches are the reports a keyword search alone would miss.

Opening a report: the Overview and Files tabs

Clicking a report’s title opens the report window. It has three tabs: Overview (the report and its key facts), Information extraction evidence (how each fact was obtained — page 4), and Files (the original documents).

FeatureWhat it does
1The three tabsOverview — the report and its key facts side by side. Information extraction evidence — how every fact was obtained, with the evidence (page 4). Files — the original documents: the main report PDF and each published response, with View and Download buttons for every file.
2The report’s factsReference, the date reported (when the coroner wrote it) and the date published (when it appeared online) — shown separately — plus a Source link that opens the report’s own page on judiciary.uk.
3The deceased & circumstancesName, age, gender and ethnicity as stated in the report — “Not stated” means exactly that; the tool never guesses. “Protected characteristics” expands to show any Equality Act facts the report states. Below: cause of death, disability or condition, drugs, category, coroner, area and NHS region — and, under the NHS category system, the report’s NHS category and its Healthcare speciality.
4Original PDF / Report textSwitch between the original document exactly as published and the Report text — the extracted text that makes the report searchable: this is what Keyword and Advanced search look through, what your search words are highlighted in, and where Ctrl + F / ⌘ + F works — including for scanned PDFs, whose original file contains no searchable text at all. The original stays alongside for comparison.
5Numbered sectionsThe report text follows the standard Regulation 28 sections (Coroner, Legal powers, Investigation, Circumstances, Concerns…), numbered for easy reference.
6Highlighted wordsYour search words are highlighted in the text — so you can see at once why this report matched your search.

User guide · the Home pageThe evidence behind every fact

The Information extraction evidence tab: where every fact comes from

This tab shows the source of every recorded fact — which part of the report it was read from, with a link to the exact sentence behind each value.

FeatureWhat it does
1Two independent readingsEach fact is read twice: a rule-based reading of the report’s published header, and a language-model reading of the whole document. Seeing both side by side shows where they agree — and where only one could find the fact.
2The “Used” columnThe value the tool actually recorded, labelled with its origin: Rule-based (from the published header — preferred when available), Language model (read from the report text), or Derived (computed, e.g. NHS region from the coroner area). The published header always takes precedence over the language model.
3Evidence linksEvery value carries its evidence: the exact quoted words and a link to the numbered sentence they appear in (e.g. “51 years old” → sentence 14), or “from the page header”. One click takes you to the sentence the fact came from.
4Page headerThe report’s header block as published on judiciary.uk — the authoritative source for reference, dates, names and area.
5Sentence segmentationThe report text split into numbered sentences by both methods, shown beside the original PDF. Clicking an evidence link highlights the exact sentence here.
6Missing values are found — and labelledWhere the website entry lacks a value, the tool fills it from the next-best source and says so. Example: many reports carried no category — the tool detects the best-fitting category from the judiciary website’s own list from the report’s own text (in the picture above: “Mental Health related deaths”, evidence → sentence 19, labelled From: Language model). A found value is always distinguishable from a published one, and a fact stated nowhere stays blank — it is never invented. And even where a category could not be detected at all, the report is still found by search: Keyword and Advanced search work on the report’s full text and meaning, not its category — a report about a topic is retrieved even if no category says so.
A worked example. Search CQC — 432 matches at the time of writing. Open one, and every mention is highlighted: in the report pictured on page 2, the concern that the CQC accepted the lack of an action plan from the provider, and never received one (pictured on page 3). Within the text, jump between words with Ctrl + F (Windows) or ⌘ + F (Mac). Files holds the original PDF and any published CQC response. The same works for any organisation, condition or circumstance — though note a common phrase like “NHS England” matches many reports through routine recipient lists; for broad questions use Advanced search instead.
Downloading the extracted data. Add reports to a Project (View / create projects in the menu) and the downloads become available: the extracted data as a shareable Excel workbook (a Dataset sheet, plus an evidence sheet for every column showing where each value came from), the factors & annotations as a CSV (the reviewed contributory factors with their supporting sentences), and a Theme annotation workbook (the reviewed themes with their evidence) — all ready for your own analysis or reporting.

Analysis & dashboard: your search, in charts

The dashboard charts whatever you are currently looking at. Your search and filters carry over from the Home page — open the dashboard after searching “home birth” and the charts show those reports (here, 52 records) until you change the search or clear the filters. Change anything here and every chart updates at once.

FeatureWhat it does
1Search — carried from HomeThe dashboard opens with the same search and filters you had on Home, so the charts describe exactly the reports you were just looking at. Type a new search, or ✕ to clear, and every chart updates.
2Select a projectLimit the whole dashboard to one of your Projects (a curated set of reports) instead of the full archive — and search and filters combine with it: select a project, then search within it. Example: project “learning disability” + Advanced search “birthday” → the 9 reports in that project matching that topic, charted. The record count at the top right always shows what is currently selected.
3FiltersThe same filters as Home: a Category system switch (Judiciary or NHS), Category, Healthcare speciality, NHS region, Coroner area, Coroner name, and date of report or date of death. Filter menus are searchable, with Clear per menu; ticks show what is selected.
4Active filtersEvery applied filter shows as a chip — remove one with its ✕, or Clear all.
5Record countEach chart states what it is counting — here “52 records across 41 months” — so you always know the scope behind the picture.
6Chart controlsDay / Week / Month sets the time granularity; Chart type switches the presentation (line / bars).
7Click a point or barOpens the list of the exact reports behind that number; click one to open the same report window as on Home. Every figure on the dashboard can be traced to its reports.
Recorded only when stated. The tool extracts more than 20 fields in all. Most — reference, dates, deceased name, coroner, area, NHS region and category — come from the published header (or are derived), so they are present for nearly every report. The following are read from the report’s own wording, so where a report does not state them they stay blank — and are therefore absent from that analysis: Date of death, Age, Gender, Ethnicity, Cause of death, disability or condition, drugs, who it was sent to (recipients), hospitals involved, and other protected characteristics (pregnancy, religion or belief…). A report is included in each analysis for which it states the detail — nothing is ever guessed to fill a gap.

The charts, and getting the data out

Six charts summarise the selected reports: over time, per year, by month, by category, by coroner area and by coroner name. All respond to the same search and filters, and each can be downloaded as a picture or as data.

FeatureWhat it does
1Label toggle & chart typeLabel: 123 shows or hides the value printed on each bar; Chart type switches between bars and a line.
2Show top …On the coroner-area and coroner-name charts, set how many are shown — the top 10, 18, 30… The heading confirms the coverage (“Showing all 18 areas”).
3Consistent countingAll charts count the same selected reports — here the 52 “home birth” records — by year, month, category, area and coroner. A report with several categories appears under each of them, which is why category counts can exceed the record count.
4Download image / CSVEvery chart exports as a picture (for slides and papers) or as CSV data (for a spreadsheet). The note by each chart flags that the exported image can differ slightly from the on-screen view.
A worked example. After the “home birth” Advanced search (192 matches), filter to the regions you cover — the charts re-draw for those records: the yearly chart shows the trend (a peak of 8 in 2023 in this example), the category chart shows most fall under Child Death and Hospital Death, and the area chart names the coroner areas involved. Click any bar to open the reports behind it; Download CSV puts the numbers straight into a spreadsheet.

This sheet — part 4 of 8 of the Lense.ai user guide — covers: Menu ▸ PROJECTS ▸ View / create projects

Projects: your own working sets of reports

A project is a named set of reports curated for a study, a review or a presentation. Each project is private to the person who created it and the colleagues it is shared with, and everything in the tool (dashboard, searches, downloads) can be scoped to one.

FeatureWhat it does
1Find, sort, trashSearch your projects by name; sort by recently updated; the bin opens Recently deleted — deleted projects wait there and can be restored, so nothing is gone for good until you empty the trash.
2Your projectsEach project is private to the person who created it and the colleagues it is shared with. Each card shows the report count, when it was last updated, the categories inside (coloured markers), and who it is shared with. A purple “N new reports” badge (e.g. “14 new reports”) flags reports the overnight update has added that match the project’s last filter, found in the activity tracker — see 6.
3Shared with youProjects colleagues have shared with you, with the sharer’s address shown.
4The ⋯ menuOpen the project in the builder; Duplicate it (a safe copy to experiment on); Share… with colleagues — note that only registered users can be invited, so a colleague must create their Lense.ai account first; Move to trash (recoverable, see 1).
5+ New projectGive the project a name and create it — that is all it takes. The project opens empty in the builder, where you add reports from the archive (next page). It is private to its creator and the colleagues it is shared with.
6Keeping a project currentA project remembers the search and filters it was last built from — its latest filter. When the overnight update adds reports that match that filter, they are flagged automatically — a purple “N new reports” badge on the project (in the list and the builder header) and a “Recently added” tag on those reports in the builder — so you simply review and add them. No manual refresh is needed, and the full history of searches is kept in Project activity (see next page).

The builder: filling and working a project

Opening a project shows two panels: the whole archive on the left, your project on the right. Search the left, add what belongs; search the right to work within what you have collected.

FeatureWhat it does
1All reports (left)Search the whole archive from inside the builder — Keyword or Advanced, with the same filters as Home. Each row carries small category tags and the report date.
2Adding reportsA pill shows how many reports the search matched (e.g. “809 matches”). Add one with its + Add button, tick several and use the Add to project bar, or + Add all for everything found. Reports the nightly update has added that match the project’s saved search are tagged Recently added, and Mark as viewed helps you track which you have checked. Reports already in the project show “✓ In project”; changes save automatically, and Undo reverses the last change.
3+ Upload PDFsBring your own documents into the project — stored privately within it, visible only to the project’s members, and processed in the background like every other report. Drag & drop or choose files, up to 20 per batch and as many batches as you like. Because that processing carries a cost, uploads are switched on per account: where they are not yet enabled the button is greyed and a note explains how to request access — contact the team to turn it on.
4In this project (right)What the project contains, with its own search box and filters — search within the project without touching the archive-wide search on the left.
5Share projectInvite colleagues by email; the count shows how many people have access, and the project appears in their “Shared with you” list. Invitations work for registered users only — ask the colleague to create an account first, then invite the address they registered with.
6Extracted metadata (.xlsx)Downloads the project’s extracted data as a shareable data-deliverable workbook. A Start here sheet explains how to read it. The Dataset sheet (blue tab) is the data — one row per report, leading with context (deceased name, coroner, area, NHS region) then every extracted field, showing not stated where the report doesn’t state a fact, never a guess. Behind it, the grey evidence-review tabs mirror the Dataset one sheet per column: each pairs the value with where it came from — the evidence sentence for a model reading, or a short note for a value taken from the page header or derived (NHS category and Healthcare speciality each get their own sheet). Every cell traces back to the report’s own words. For a large project a “Large export” progress note shows the report count while the workbook is prepared. A project’s second export — Factors & annotations (.csv), the reviewed contributory factors with their evidence — is available from the Factor annotation page.
A worked example. Create a project, add the matches for an Advanced search — say “learning disability”. From then on, reports the nightly update adds that match are flagged automatically — a purple “new reports” badge and a “Recently added” tag — so you just review and add them. Duplicate project makes a safe copy to experiment on, and Project activity keeps a dated history of who changed what — the searches and filters each member applied, and the reports they added or removed — with any past search re-openable through Open. Share the project with a colleague, scope the dashboard to it for the charts, and click Extracted metadata (.xlsx) when you need the data in a spreadsheet.

Factor annotation: confirming the contributory factors

Every report is annotated automatically the night it is scraped: the tool identifies which Yorkshire Contributory Factors are present, each tied to the sentences that support it. This page is where a person can review and edit them if they wish — accept, reject or relabel each factor. The archive and analyses use these annotations, with any human corrections applied.

FeatureWhat it does
1Project & progressChoose the project to review. The header keeps you oriented: which document you are on (doc 1/284), how many factors it carries (6 factors), and how much reviewing remains across the project (“1411 left to review”).
2Export projectTwo exports are available from this page. Factors & annotations (.csv): one row per factor per document — the tool’s suggested code and factor, the human-reviewed code, factor and decision, a confidence band and score, and the supporting sentence numbers with the sentences themselves. Extracted metadata (.xlsx): the project’s data-deliverable workbook — a Dataset sheet plus an evidence sheet for every column (described in the Projects guide).
3Sentences / Original PDFThe report as numbered sentences (what the factor evidence points into), or the original document as published — switch freely while reviewing.
4Annotators & reviewed countShows who tagged the factors on this report — the Human reviewer or the Lense.AI annotator (the tool), each with its own count — and how many of the document’s factors you have reviewed so far (“1 of 6”). A Click here for guidance on how to annotate link opens the annotation guidance.
5The factors, with evidenceEach identified Yorkshire Contributory Factor (colour-coded by framework tier) expands to show the exact sentence(s) supporting it, with the sentence number (¶ 42).
6Accept / Reject / RelabelYour decision on each factor: Accept it (one supporting sentence is enough), Reject it only when nothing in the report supports it, or Relabel the whole factor when its label is wrong for all of its sentences. The published report is never altered — annotation is a separate layer, and the archive and analyses use the current labels, with any human corrections applied.
7Compare approachesView the Human reviewer’s annotations and the Lense.AI annotator’s side by side on the same report — so you can see exactly where the human review agreed with, added to, or corrected the tool’s suggestions.
8Highlighted evidence in the textEvery sentence that supports a factor is tinted in the report itself, ending in a small square in the factor’s colour; the sentence belonging to the factor you are reviewing is emphasised. You read the evidence exactly where it sits in the report.
9Decided factorsA reviewed factor carries its decision mark (✓), and the reviewed count updates — a reviewed document reads at a glance.
Important — how to review each factor. Read the supporting sentences collectively; the task is not to agree or disagree with each sentence in isolation, and if a sentence seems mislabelled, read it in the context of the report first. 1. Accept if at least one sentence supports the factor — one sentence is enough, because the analysis records only whether a factor is present or absent in a report, not how many sentences support it. 2. Add anything missing: if a sentence in the report supports a factor that was not suggested, click that sentence and select its factor. 3. Relabel with care — relabelling changes the whole factor, not one sentence, so relabel only when the label is wrong for all its sentences; for a single misplaced sentence, use step 2 instead. 4. Reject only when nothing supports it — not because some suggested sentences are wrong, or because you mean to add a better one. Work through documents with Prev / Next; progress saves as you go.
Coming up. The ability for a reviewer to remove individual sentences from a contributory factor — functionality planned for September 2026.

Factor analysis: the contributory factors in charts

This page aggregates the Yorkshire Contributory Factors across the selected reports: which factors occur most, how each has moved over time, and how they distribute across the report categories and coroner areas. A Category system toggle (Judiciary or NHS) sits at the top of the page — switch it and every category-based chart re-groups to match. Every chart downloads as an image or CSV.

FeatureWhat it does
1Factors over the yearsA heatmap of every Yorkshire Contributory Factor by year (2013 onwards): each cell shows the share — and count — of that year’s reports carrying the factor. Toggle percentages and counts; narrow by year or by factor category (tier); the Total column gives each factor’s overall report count.
2Factors rankedThe factors ordered by how many reports they appear in, with the share labelled (e.g. Care Planning in 61.6% of reports). Bars are coloured by framework tier — Situational, Communication & Culture, Local working conditions, Organisational, External, Human — so the balance of tiers reads at a glance.
3Trend in a factorPick a factor and see the reports carrying it over time, grouped by month or year, with an optional trend line — the quickest way to see whether a factor is rising or fading.
4Factors by report categoryFor each report category, the mix of factor tiers as a 100% stacked bar — showing, for example, whether hospital deaths carry proportionally more organisational factors than road deaths. The Category system toggle switches the rows between the Judiciary categories and the NHS categories, so the same factors can be read against whichever framework you work in.
5Factors by coroner area — or NHS regionThe factor × area matrix: each cell is the share and count of an area’s reports carrying the factor, with an area drop-down to narrow it. A Group by NHS region toggle rolls the coroner areas up into the seven NHS England regions — showing where each factor concentrates geographically, at whichever level you need.
6DownloadsEvery panel exports as an image (for slides and papers) or CSV (for a spreadsheet), like the rest of the tool; the interactive factor network view also exports as a self-contained HTML file you can open and explore in a browser.
Where the numbers come from. These charts count the contributory factors identified on each report and reviewed on the Factor annotation page — each factor tied to the sentences that support it. A factor is counted once per report — the counts reflect whether it is present or absent, not how many times (or in how many sentences) it appears in that report. The factor set is the Yorkshire Contributory Factors Framework; letters mark the tier (C, E, H, L, O, S).

This sheet — part 7 of 8 of the Lense.ai user guide — covers: Menu ▸ THEMATIC ANALYSIS ▸ Theme annotation

Theme annotation: agreeing the themes found in each report

Beyond the fixed contributory-factor framework, the tool also finds themes in each report’s own wording — recurring subjects, each with a one-line summary and its supporting sentences. This too is done automatically as each report is scraped; here a person can accept or reject each theme for the project if they wish.

FeatureWhat it does
1Project & progressChoose the project. The header shows the report you are on (report 1/284), how many themes it carries, and what remains project-wide (“811 left to review”). How it works opens a short explanation.
2Theme annotation workbookDownloads an Excel workbook of the project’s themes, grouped by cluster: one row per theme with the report it came from, the theme name, its one-line summary, the evidence sentences and their count. You can rename any theme in the workbook as you wish for your own reporting.
3Themes in this reportThe themes found in this report’s own wording, each with a one-line summary and its evidence sentences (¶ 16, ¶ 25…). Theme names are kept neutral — they name the subject matter without characterising it positively or negatively.
4Evidence in the textThe supporting sentences are highlighted in the report itself, colour-matched to their theme — you judge each theme against the report’s own words, in context.
5Accept / Reject, with a noteYour decision on each theme for this project — Accept when at least one sentence supports it, Reject only when nothing does — with an optional note to record your reasoning. The reviewed count updates as you go, and decisions can be changed at any time.
6Status at a glanceEach theme carries its decision chip — ✓ accepted or ✕ rejected — so a reviewed report reads at a glance. Rejected themes are excluded from the Theme analysis; the automatic and accepted themes feed it.
Important — how to review each theme. Read the supporting sentences collectively. Accept the theme if at least one sentence supports it — one sentence is enough, because the analysis records only whether a theme is present or absent in a report, not how many sentences support it. Reject only when nothing in the report supports it — not because some suggested sentences are wrong, or because you mean to add a better one. Decisions are per project and can be changed at any time; to download your work, use the Theme annotation workbook link.
Coming up. The ability for a reviewer to add and remove the sentences under a theme, and to add new themes — functionality planned for September 2026.

Theme analysis: the themes across the whole selection

Theme analysis aggregates the reviewed themes across every selected report: which themes occur most, and how they distribute across the report categories. A Category system toggle (Judiciary or NHS) re-groups the category charts, and the familiar filters (Healthcare speciality, NHS region, coroner area and name, date of report or death) apply throughout.

FeatureWhat it does
1Grouped clustersThemes with similar meaning are grouped into clusters, each with its report count (“Clinical Communication (31)”). Select one to explore the themes and reports inside it.
2Top themesHow many reports carry each theme cluster, most frequent first. Downloads as an image (for slides) or CSV (for a spreadsheet), like every chart in the tool.
3Trend over timePick a theme cluster and see how many reports carry it each year — the quickest way to spot a theme that is rising or fading.
4Theme co-occurrence matrixWhich themes appear together in the same reports: each cell counts the reports carrying both themes (darker = more); the muted diagonal is each theme’s own total; hover a cell for the exact pair. Useful for seeing which issues travel together.
5Themes by category — and by coroner areaHow the themes distribute across the report categories — each cell shows the share and count of a category’s reports carrying that theme, with percentage/count toggles and a Top-N control. The Category system toggle switches the rows between the Judiciary and NHS categories. The same matrix is available by coroner area, with NHS region and area drop-downs, showing where each theme concentrates geographically.
6Review listEvery report in the project with its theme count and review progress (“0/4 reviewed”), each with an Open report link straight into Theme annotation — the working to-do list for the reviewing effort.
About theme names. Theme names are kept neutral: they name the subject matter (“Clinical Communication”, “Care Documentation”) without characterising it positively or negatively. And the names are yours to adapt: download the Theme annotation workbook and rename any theme to suit your own reporting.
